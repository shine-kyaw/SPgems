Mogok Ruby

Ruby is the material this house was built on. We recover it from our own workings in the Mogok valley, buy it as rough from miners we have dealt with for years, and cut it ourselves in Yangon. Mogok ruby has a specific gemmological signature — low iron, a strong red fluorescence, fine rutile silk, a swirl-like growth structure — and that signature is what allows a laboratory to call the origin rather than guess it.

About Mogok ruby

Why the valley matters to the price

Ruby is corundum coloured red by chromium. What makes Mogok material distinct is what is absent from it: iron. Iron quenches fluorescence, so ruby from iron-richer deposits sits quieter under light. Mogok ruby, low in iron, fluoresces red under the same ultraviolet component that is present in daylight — which is why a good Mogok stone appears to glow from within rather than merely reflect.

That is a measurable property, not a marketing line, and it is one of the features a laboratory looks for when it issues an origin opinion. It is also why the market pays a premium for Mogok over the broader Burma designation, and a further premium for material a laboratory has confirmed as unheated.

Sizes matter disproportionately here. Fine unheated Mogok ruby above three carats is genuinely scarce; above five carats it is a several-year event rather than an annual one. Most of what the valley produces is smaller, and much of it is heated to improve colour and clarity — legitimately, and always disclosed on these pages.
