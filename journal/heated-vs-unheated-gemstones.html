Heated vs unheated: what gemstone treatment actually means

The question we are asked most often, answered without a sales agenda. Heating is legitimate. It must always be disclosed. And it is not the same thing as being inferior.

SP GEMS Article hero Rough ruby, before and after heating If SP Gems can photograph the same parcel before and after treatment, that is a genuinely rare illustration. Photography pending

At a glance

  • Most ruby and sapphire on the market is heated. This has been normal practice for centuries.
  • Heating improves colour and clarity permanently. It is stable and requires no special care.
  • It must always be disclosed. Non-disclosure, not the treatment, is the ethical problem.
  • Unheated stones of comparable quality are much rarer, and price reflects that — often by a multiple.
  • “Unheated” on a poor stone is worth less than “heated” on a fine one. Treatment is one variable, not a verdict.

A seller with an unheated stone has a commercial incentive to imply that heated stones are damaged goods. A seller with heated stock has the opposite incentive. Since we sell both, here is the version we would give a gemmologist.

What heating does

Corundum — the mineral of both ruby and sapphire — frequently comes out of the ground with two problems. Some of the chromium or iron and titanium responsible for colour is chemically locked up in a form that does not contribute to the colour you see. And the stone often contains rutile silk: fine needles of titanium dioxide that scatter light and can make the material look cloudy or sleepy.

Heating to somewhere between roughly 800 °C and 1,800 °C, depending on the material and the intended result, addresses both. It redistributes trace elements into positions where they do contribute to colour, and it dissolves silk back into the lattice. The result is a stone with stronger, more even colour and better transparency.

Three things are worth understanding about this. It is permanent — the stone does not revert. It is stable — a heated stone needs no special handling and behaves like any other. And it is not additive: nothing is put into the stone. Its own chemistry is rearranged.

Why it is normal rather than shameful

Heat treatment of corundum has been practised in one form or another for something like two thousand years, and industrially for well over a century. It is accepted by every major gemmological laboratory, by the ICA and AGTA, and by the trade as a whole.

The reason is arithmetic. If heated material were excluded from the market, fine ruby and sapphire would become the preserve of a very small number of buyers, and almost nobody would own a good one. Heating is what makes the category exist at the scale it does.

The ethical requirement is not that a stone be untreated. It is that the treatment be disclosed, accurately, every time. Non-disclosure is the offence.

Where the line moves: residues and fillers

Not all heat treatment is the same, and this is where a buyer needs to pay attention.

  • Conventional heating. Heat alone, no additives. Reported as heated. Universally accepted, and the great majority of what is on the market.
  • Heating with residue. Fluxes such as borax are sometimes used during heating and can leave glassy residue in surface-reaching fissures. Laboratories report this separately, and the market prices it lower than conventional heating.
  • Lead-glass filling. Heavily fractured, low-grade material infused with high-refractive-index lead glass to mask the fractures. This is a different category of thing: it is not stable, it can be damaged by ordinary jewellery repair and by household chemicals, and it is worth a small fraction of even modest untreated material. It must be disclosed and it frequently is not.
  • Diffusion and irradiation. Colour introduced from outside the stone, or altered by radiation. Separate treatments with separate disclosure requirements and separate values.

When someone says a stone is “treated,” that word covers all of the above. Ask which.

What the premium actually is

For fine Burmese ruby and sapphire, an unheated stone with a laboratory report confirming no heat commonly trades at a substantial multiple of an otherwise comparable heated stone. The multiple widens as size and quality rise, because the pool of candidates narrows so sharply at the top.

But the comparison has to be like for like, and this is where buyers most often go wrong. An unheated stone with weak colour, poor transparency and a sloppy cut is not more valuable than a well-cut, vivid, transparent heated stone. It is less valuable, and usually much less. Treatment is one variable in a set that includes colour, clarity, cut, size and origin. It is not a verdict on the stone.

The practical implication for a buyer with a fixed budget is worth stating directly: at most budgets, insisting on “unheated” means accepting a smaller or less fine stone. Whether that is the right trade depends on whether you are buying to wear or to hold.

How treatment is determined

A gemmologist looks at the inclusions. Heating leaves traces: rutile silk shows signs of having partially dissolved, mineral inclusions may show tension halos where they expanded against the host, and fluid inclusions are altered or destroyed. Advanced laboratories add spectroscopy to the picture.

Two honest caveats. Very lightly heated stones can be genuinely difficult to call, and reputable laboratories will sometimes report the result as inconclusive rather than guess. And a laboratory report states a finding, not a guarantee — which is why the laboratory's own standing matters, and why we publish report numbers openly so you can verify them at the source rather than take our word for it.

How we handle it

Every stone in our catalogue carries a dedicated treatment section — visually distinct, on the page whether the stone is heated or not, never a footnote. We state the status in plain language, and we state separately whether the finding is confirmed by a laboratory or is our own assessment.

Making that section prominent for treated stones is the point. A disclosure that only appears when the news is good is not a disclosure, and a buyer can tell the difference.

Terms used here

  • Rutile silk — fine titanium dioxide needle inclusions; dissolved by heating.
  • Flux — a substance used during heating that can leave glassy residue in fissures.
  • Diffusion — colour introduced into the surface layers from outside the stone.
  • No heat / unheated — a laboratory finding that no thermal enhancement is detectable.
