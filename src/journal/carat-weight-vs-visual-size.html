Carat weight vs visual size

A carat is a unit of weight, not of size. Two stones of identical carat weight can look a full size apart on the hand — and the heavier one is often the worse buy.

At a glance

  • A carat is 0.2 grams. It measures mass, and says nothing directly about how large a stone looks.
  • Weight hidden in a deep pavilion does not show face-up. You paid for it and you cannot see it.
  • Millimetre dimensions tell you the visible size. Always ask for all three.
  • Different species have different densities: a 3 ct spinel looks larger than a 3 ct ruby.
  • Some depth is necessary — too shallow and the stone windows. The question is whether the depth is doing work.

Buyers arrive with a carat number in mind, almost always. It is the one gemstone unit that has entered ordinary language. It is also the one most likely to mislead you about what you are going to see on your hand.

What a carat is

One carat is 0.2 grams, standardised internationally in the early twentieth century. It is a measure of mass. Nothing about it describes how wide a stone is, how deep, or how large it looks from above.

For a rough intuition of how quickly weight and size diverge: doubling the linear dimensions of a stone multiplies its weight by roughly eight. Weight scales with volume, and volume scales with the cube. Which means small differences in depth translate into surprisingly large differences in weight — weight the buyer pays for and never sees.

Where the weight hides

A cut stone has a crown above the girdle and a pavilion below it. When a stone is set, the pavilion is inside the setting. Nobody looks at it.

A cutter working from a piece of rough that happens to be deep has a choice. Cut to ideal proportions and lose weight to the wheel, or leave the extra depth in the pavilion and keep it. Since rough is bought by weight and cut stones are sold by weight, the commercial incentive points firmly at keeping it.

The result is common enough to have its own shorthand in the trade: a heavy stone that “faces up small.” Two rubies at 3.00 ct, one cut to good proportions and one carrying a deep pavilion, can differ by a millimetre or more across the table. On a ring, that is visible at conversational distance.

So ask for the millimetres

Length, width and depth, to two decimal places. This is why our catalogue publishes all three on every stone, and why serious collectors treat a listing without them as incomplete.

The first two numbers tell you what you will see. The third tells you where the weight went. A stone whose depth is disproportionate to its width is carrying weight you are paying for and cannot look at.

The useful comparison is between two specific stones, not against a table of ideal ratios — coloured stones are cut to the colour of the individual piece of rough, and rigid proportion rules borrowed from diamond cutting do not transfer.

The opposite error

Depth is not simply waste. A stone cut too shallow develops a window: a transparent patch through the middle where light passes straight through instead of returning to the eye. Windowed stones look washed out and pale in the centre, and no amount of favourable face-up measurement compensates.

In some material, depth is doing specific work. Burmese sapphire's velvety character depends on light travelling far enough through the stone to interact with its internal silk; open the stone up too much and that character disappears. Our cutters will accept a slightly deep pavilion in sapphire for exactly this reason, and it is a deliberate decision rather than a weight grab.

The question is never “is this stone deep?” It is “is the depth doing anything?”

Species matters too

Different gem species have different densities, so the same carat weight occupies different volumes.

Corundum — ruby and sapphire — is relatively dense, at roughly 4.0. Spinel is lighter, around 3.6. So a 3 ct spinel is a physically larger object than a 3 ct ruby cut to the same proportions, by a noticeable margin. Peridot, lighter still, is larger again.

If you are buying to a visual size rather than to a number, this is worth knowing before you fix on a carat weight. It is also one of several reasons Mogok spinel offers so much more visible stone per dollar than ruby does.

What to do with all this

  • Decide whether you are buying a number or an object. Both are legitimate; they lead to different stones.
  • Always get all three dimensions in millimetres, to two decimal places.
  • Compare face-up dimensions between candidate stones, not carat weights.
  • Ask to see the stone from the side. A profile photograph shows the pavilion immediately.
  • Ask whether the stone windows, and ask for an image over a light background.

How we handle it

Dimensions to two decimal places on every stone, always, in the key specifications table. Our photography includes an angled three-quarter view that shows the crown and pavilion transition, and a scale reference — the stone in tweezers or on a hand — because no measurement communicates size as immediately as seeing it held.

Where we have accepted extra depth in the cut, that is a decision we can explain, and the person who made it works down the corridor from the person who will explain it.

Terms used here

  • Carat — 0.2 grams. A measure of mass, not size.
  • Crown / pavilion — the parts of a cut stone above and below the girdle.
  • Window — a see-through patch in a stone cut too shallow.
  • Faces up — how the stone appears viewed from above, as it is worn.
