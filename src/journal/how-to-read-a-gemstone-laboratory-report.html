How to read a gemstone laboratory report

A field guide to the document that decides the price. What each section is actually asserting, what it deliberately is not, and how to check that the report in front of you is real.

SP GEMS Article hero A laboratory report, photographed at an angle Use a real report from our own inventory, watermarked, at a resolution insufficient to be passed off as an original. Photography pending

At a glance

  • A report identifies a stone and states findings. It is not a valuation and not a guarantee.
  • Some findings are measurements. Others — origin, top colour grades — are expert opinions.
  • Read the treatment line first. It usually moves the price more than anything else on the page.
  • Check the report number at the laboratory’s own site. Never at the seller’s.
  • Confirm the dimensions and weight on the report match the stone in front of you.

For fine coloured stones the laboratory report is the closest thing to an objective document in the transaction. It is also routinely misread, in both directions: treated as gospel by buyers who do not know its limits, and dismissed as paperwork by sellers who would rather you looked at the stone.

What a report is

A report is a laboratory's statement, on a given date, about a specific stone that was physically in its hands. It identifies the material, records measurements, and states findings on treatment and often on geographic origin.

It is worth being clear about three things it is not. It is not a valuation — no reputable laboratory prices stones. It is not a guarantee or a warranty. And it is not transferable to another stone, which is why the measurements matter.

Section by section

Identification

“Natural ruby” or “natural sapphire.” The word natural is doing real work: it distinguishes the stone from a synthetic, which is chemically identical corundum grown in a factory. This part is a measurement, and it is not in doubt.

Weight and dimensions

Carat weight to two decimal places, and dimensions in millimetres, usually to two decimal places as well. This is the section that ties the report to the object. Measure the stone. If the report says 9.42 × 7.88 × 4.96 mm and the stone in front of you is visibly a different size, you are looking at the wrong stone or the wrong report.

Shape and cut

Descriptive. Note that laboratories do not grade cut quality in coloured stones the way they do in diamonds. A report will tell you a stone is a cushion mixed cut. It will not tell you whether it was cut well. That judgment stays with you and with the photography.

Colour

Two different things can appear here. A descriptive colour — “red,” “blue” — which is straightforward. And, on some reports from some laboratories, a trade colour grade such as “Pigeon's Blood” or “Royal Blue,” assessed against that laboratory's own master stones.

Trade colour grades are laboratory-specific and not interchangeable between laboratories. Discussed at length here.

Treatment — read this first

Usually the single most price-relevant line on the page. The vocabulary varies but the categories are consistent:

  • “No indications of heating” or “unheated” — the finding you pay a premium for.
  • “Indications of heating” — conventionally heated. Accepted, and the majority of the market.
  • “Indications of heating with residues in fissures” — flux residue present. A materially lower value than clean heating.
  • “Inconclusive” — the laboratory could not call it. An honest answer, and one that affects price.
  • Diffusion, irradiation, glass filling and clarity enhancement each have their own wording, and each is a different category of thing.

Note the phrasing: indications of. A laboratory is reporting what it observed, not asserting a history it cannot have witnessed. That is careful language, not hedging.

Origin

Not every report includes it, and it usually costs extra. When present it is an opinion — a comparative judgment against the laboratory's reference collection of known-origin material, expressed with a confidence level.

For Burmese material the distinction between “Mogok” and the broader “Burma (Myanmar)” is commercially significant. Some reports state the country; some state the valley. They are not the same claim. Why that matters.

Comments

The most-skipped section and often the most informative. Additional observations live here: unusual inclusions, the basis for an origin call, notes on the stone's optical behaviour. Read it.

How to verify a report

Reports are forged, and photoshopped scans circulate. Four checks, in order of value:

  • Look it up at the laboratory. Most major laboratories offer online report verification. Type the number into the laboratory's own website — not a link the seller sends you, which is trivially faked. This is the check that matters.
  • Measure the stone. Weight and dimensions should match. A report legitimately issued for a different stone is the commonest substitution.
  • Check the date. An old report on a stone that has since been recut, reset, or retreated is describing something that no longer exists in that form.
  • Ask for the original. For a significant purchase, the physical document should accompany the stone.

Which laboratories

For Burmese coloured stones the names you will most often see are GIA, GRS, AGL, Lotus, SSEF, Gübelin and AIGS. They are not interchangeable: they differ in the depth of their reference collections for particular origins, in their colour-grading vocabulary, in turnaround, and in cost.

The absence of a report is not automatically a warning sign, particularly on smaller or lower-value stones where a report can cost a meaningful fraction of the stone's value. What is a warning sign is a seller who is vague about whether a report exists.

How we handle it

Every certified stone in our catalogue publishes its laboratory name and its report number openly, in text, on the page. That is deliberate: a report number is of no use to a competitor, and it lets you verify our claim at the laboratory instead of trusting us. Where a laboratory offers online verification, we link to it directly.

The full scan is released on enquiry rather than published, because the scan carries our own identity and is the asset most often copied in this trade. Where a stone has no report, we say so plainly on its page rather than staying silent, because silence on certification reads as concealment.

Terms used here

  • Natural — formed in the earth, as opposed to synthetic.
  • Indications of heating — observed evidence consistent with thermal treatment.
  • Origin opinion — a comparative judgment on geographic source, with a stated confidence.
  • Master stones — reference specimens used for colour grading.
