“Pigeon’s blood”: what the term means, and who can use it

One of the most misused phrases in the trade. It is a laboratory colour grade, not an adjective — and the difference between those two things is worth money.

At a glance

  • Originally a Burmese trade expression for the finest red, with no fixed definition.
  • Now a formal colour grade issued by particular laboratories against their own reference standards.
  • “Pigeon’s Blood (GRS)” means something specific. “Pigeon’s blood” in a seller’s description means nothing.
  • Different laboratories set the bar differently. The name of the laboratory is part of the claim.
  • If nobody has issued it, nobody should be saying it. We never apply the term editorially.

Search for ruby online and you will find the phrase everywhere: on stones worth six figures and on stones worth two hundred dollars. That is your first clue that most uses of it carry no information.

Where the term came from

Ko-twe — pigeon's blood — is an old Burmese trade expression for the finest red the Mogok valley produces. Like most trade language it was descriptive and local: an experienced sorter used it to mean a particular saturated red with a slight blue undertone, understood by reference to other stones on the table rather than to any written standard.

Various romantic derivations circulate — the colour of the first two drops of blood from a slaughtered pigeon, the colour of a Burmese pigeon's eye. None is documented well enough to repeat as fact, and it does not matter. What matters is that for most of the term's life it had no definition at all, only a consensus among people who handled the material.

What happened next

As Burmese ruby moved onto the international market, the phrase moved with it — and detached from the people who knew what it meant. By the late twentieth century it was being applied to almost any red ruby by almost anyone.

Laboratories responded by formalising it. GRS in particular established “Pigeon's Blood” as a defined colour grade, assessed against master reference stones under controlled lighting, and issued on a report. Other laboratories have their own top-colour designations, expressed in their own vocabulary, against their own references.

So the phrase now leads a double life. On a laboratory report it is a technical grade with a procedure behind it. In a seller's own description it is marketing.

Why the laboratory name is part of the claim

There is no cross-laboratory standard for top red. Each laboratory maintains its own reference set and its own threshold, and they do not align exactly. A stone can receive a top-colour designation from one laboratory and fall just short at another, without anybody having done anything wrong.

This is why an unattributed colour term is close to worthless, and why a properly stated one always names its source and its report: Colour graded Pigeon's Blood by GRS, report number so-and-so.

It is also why the designation should never be read as a substitute for looking at the stone. A colour grade compresses a great deal into two words. Two stones sharing the grade can differ visibly in tone, in how they behave under incandescent light, and in how much of the face is actually carrying the colour.

A note on where it does not apply

The equivalent for sapphire is “Royal Blue,” which has travelled the same road from trade shorthand to laboratory grade, and carries the same caveats.

Both terms are for corundum. They are occasionally seen applied to spinel, which is a different mineral with a different colour behaviour. That is a misuse, and a fairly reliable indicator of a seller working from a price list rather than from the stones.

What to ask

  • Which laboratory issued the term, and what is the report number? If the answer is neither, the term is decoration.
  • Can I see the report? The number should be public; the scan is commonly released on enquiry, which is reasonable.
  • How does it behave in different light? Ask for daylight and incandescent images, or look on a video call.
  • Is it heated? Colour grade and treatment are separate findings. A heated stone can carry a top colour grade. Covered here.

How we handle it

We state a trade colour term only where a laboratory has issued it, and we always state which laboratory and under which report. Where no laboratory has issued one, our stones carry a plain-language colour description — “vivid red, slight purplish modifier” — and nothing more.

We could put “pigeon's blood” on considerably more of our inventory than we do. We would rather the term meant something on the stones where we use it.

Terms used here

  • Trade colour term — a named colour designation issued by a laboratory against its own reference standards.
  • Hue, tone, saturation — the three components of colour description: which colour, how light or dark, how intense.
  • Master stones — reference specimens a laboratory grades against.
