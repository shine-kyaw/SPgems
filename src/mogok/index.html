Upper Myanmar

Mogok

A fold of marble in the hills north-east of Mandalay, worked continuously for at least four hundred years, which produced the stone the rest of the world measures red against.

Almost every house selling Burmese material talks about Mogok. Very few of them have been there in the last year, and fewer still have taken anything out of the ground. We work there. These pages are what that means in practice.

The valley

Four hundred years of records, and a chemistry that explains the price

The earliest surviving records of organised mining at Mogok date from 1597. The deposits are unusual: ruby and spinel occur together in metamorphosed marble rather than in the basalt-hosted deposits that supply most of the world's corundum. That host rock is the reason for everything that follows.

Marble-hosted corundum forms in an environment poor in iron. Iron quenches fluorescence. Mogok ruby, low in iron and high in chromium, fluoresces red under the ultraviolet component present in ordinary daylight — so it appears to emit light rather than merely reflect it. Add fine rutile silk and a characteristic swirl-like internal growth structure and you have a gemmological fingerprint specific enough for a laboratory to state an origin on.

That is why “Mogok” is a price and not just a place name, and it is why we publish the origin region on every stone rather than a vague country.
