Burmese Sapphire

Mogok sapphire is judged on different terms from the Kashmir and Ceylon material that dominates the conversation. Its character is velvety rather than glassy — a softness caused by fine internal silk that scatters light through the stone. That is the quality the trade actually pays for here, and it is the reason our cutters will accept a slightly deeper pavilion rather than open a stone up and lose it.

About Burmese sapphire

Velvet, not glass

Sapphire is the same mineral as ruby — corundum — coloured blue by iron and titanium rather than red by chromium. Mogok produces it from the same marble-hosted system, and the material carries the same fine rutile silk that gives Mogok ruby its character. In sapphire, that silk does something specific: it diffuses light and produces the velvety appearance the market associates with fine Burmese blue.

Too much silk and the stone goes sleepy; too little and it reads as ordinary. The judgment sits in between, and it is a judgment that has to be made on the stone rather than from a specification sheet — which is why we shoot these stones under both daylight-balanced and incandescent light, and why we will always show you one on camera before you commit.

Where silk is dense and oriented, the material becomes star sapphire. Those stones are cut as cabochons and oriented so the six-rayed star sits square to the dome. Dome height is set to centre the star rather than to maximise weight, which costs carats and is the right trade.
