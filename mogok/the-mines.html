The mines

The Mogok Stone Tract runs roughly north-east from Mandalay across about a thousand square kilometres of folded, metamorphosed limestone. This is where our material comes from.

The geology, briefly

Most of the world's ruby and sapphire comes out of basalt-hosted deposits — volcanic rock, iron-rich. Mogok is different. Here corundum formed inside marble, as limestone was cooked and folded during the collision that raised the Himalaya. Marble is poor in iron and, at Mogok, locally rich in chromium.

That single fact drives the commercial character of the whole deposit. Chromium supplies red. The absence of iron leaves the fluorescence intact — iron quenches it — so a Mogok ruby responds to the ultraviolet component of daylight and appears lit from within. The same marble system produces spinel alongside ruby, which is why the two were traded as one commodity for a thousand years, and peridot, tourmaline and moonstone in the surrounding rock.

For a buyer, the practical consequence is that origin is determinable. Fine rutile silk, a swirl-like internal growth structure, low iron and strong red fluorescence together make a fingerprint a laboratory will state an opinion on. That is why we publish the region rather than the country, and why we distinguish on every stone between an origin a laboratory has confirmed and one that is our own assessment.

How the material reaches us

Three routes, and we tell you which one applied to any given stone:

  • Our own workings in Mogok. Recovered by us. This is the material where we can document origin from the ground, and it is marked on the stone record.
  • Independent miners in Mogok. Rough bought at the point of production from people we have dealt with across many seasons, and recorded at purchase.
  • Namyarseik, Kachin State. A separate relationship, and mostly spinel. Kachin material is not Mogok material and we do not describe it as such.

What mining here actually looks like

Not what the word suggests. Mogok is worked by a mix of methods: shallow pits following the gem-bearing gravels, tunnels driven into the hillside, and the washing and sorting of loose material by hand. It is slow, and the ratio of rock moved to stone recovered is the reason fine Burmese material costs what it does.

The sorting table is where the expertise sits. A piece of rough has to be judged for colour it does not yet show, for clarity hidden behind a skin, and for how much of its weight will survive being made into a shape. Get that wrong and the stone is worth a fraction of what it should have been. We do that sorting ourselves.
